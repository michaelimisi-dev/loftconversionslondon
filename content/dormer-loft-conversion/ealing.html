Local knowledge

What Makes Dormer Conversions in Ealing Different

Ealing grew in two waves. The first, from the 1870s to the First World War, filled Acton, central Ealing, West Ealing and Hanwell with terraces and villas close to the railway. The second, between the wars, spread semi-detached estates across Greenford, Perivale and Northolt. Terraces usually take a straightforward rear dormer; on the 1930s semis, a dormer is often combined with a hip-to-gable.

Ealing's residential extensions guidance is clear about the dormer itself: it should not appear unduly obtrusive or dominant, it should respect the character of the original house, and it should not take up the entire area of the roof slope.1 That means planning applications should show a dormer set in from the edges of the roof, not a box filling the whole slope. The guidance also points to rooflights as a good alternative where they give enough headroom.

Planning in Ealing

Planning a Dormer in Ealing

  • Permitted development: a rear dormer within 40 cubic metres on a terrace or 50 on other houses often needs no application. Ealing recommends a lawful development certificate to confirm it.1, 2
  • Conservation areas: permitted development rights may not apply, and roof extensions must be especially sensitively designed.
  • Brentham Garden Suburb: designated in 1969, with an Article 4 direction since 1976 that removes virtually all permitted development rights.3
