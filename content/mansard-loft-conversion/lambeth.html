Local knowledge

Lambeth's London Roofs and Mansards

Many early to mid-19th century houses in Lambeth have a "London roof": two pitches sloping into a central valley, hidden behind a front parapet so the roof is barely visible from the street. Lambeth's Design Guide calls these a key part of London's local distinctiveness.1 They are common in the older northern parts of the borough, such as Kennington, Vauxhall and Stockwell.

Because they are so valued, Lambeth does not accept the loss of a London roof in a conservation area or on a heritage asset. Elsewhere, it will only support replacing one with a traditional full mansard: two 70 degree lower slopes, two 30 degree upper slopes, an internal head height of 2.1 metres, and the existing front parapet kept at its current height.1 On later Victorian terraces with ordinary double-pitched roofs, a rear mansard is the council's preferred way to add space without touching the street-facing roof.

Planning in Lambeth

Lambeth's Rear Mansard Rules

For rear mansards, Lambeth's Design Guide asks designers to:1

  • Make the lower slope 70 degrees, hung with slate.
  • End the 70 degree slope at 2.3 metres where mansards along a row are meant to line up.
  • Avoid fascias and gutters at the junction between the two slopes.
  • Build party wall upstands in matching brick with a brick-on-edge coping.
  • Not alter ridges on pairs or groups of houses that share a ridge line.

A planning application is needed for almost every mansard. In conservation areas, Lambeth generally considers rear mansards inappropriate, and a modest dormer is usually the realistic option.
